<Tbx>
	<p>
		These opening lines come straight from that place in me where decades of
		swallowing rage have calcified into something like an ulcer that occasionally
		whispers, “Say it now or die constipated forever.” I grew up learning not to
		make noise—not because I was noble but because the school toilets of North
		Calcutta trained me in a brutal curriculum of shame where every bodily urge
		was either embarrassing or hazardous. You learn to be silent first with your
		bowels and then with your politics. And now, in my fifties, I can feel the
		way silence corrodes the spine, the same way the public loos corroded my
		ability to trust any surface, wall, or adult.
	</p>
	<p>
		The whole Sisyphus thing is not an academic flourish. It’s me remembering
		how every election cycle feels like watching a trolley full of slogans and
		swami-blessed cattle manure pushed up a slope by people who have already
		forgotten what crushed them the last time. And the “ball cadavers”—I confess
		that’s autobiographical spite. I’ve been that neutered creature, swaggering
		around with my teenage testosterone and a head emptier than the promises
		pasted on political posters. I have loved holy excuses too: I’ve seen my
		kith and kin call god to explain away every human nefariousness.
	</p>
	<p>
		When I wrote “shit your pants” I remembered my own two catastrophic
		Calcutta rickshaw rides when my digestive system announced
		it was sovereign and immune to school schedules. The small boy trying to keep dignity while everything warm and
		unspeakable spread inside his half-pants. I now see the
		same helplessness in grown-up India—millions shitting their political
		futures but calling it divine will, millions too frightened or tired to
		scream when the country wipes its sins with our silence.
	</p>
	<p>
		So yes, beneath this stanza is an old, gentle fear:
		that if I actually scream, the whole mythology-sanitized nation will turn
		and tell me to shut up and pray. The same way the shopkeeper at College
		Street told me to lug a filthy balti myself and solve the problem quietly.
		India has always demanded that we disinfect its mess with our private
		shame. I’m just refusing to do that anymore, at my own peril. 
	</p>

	<p>
		This second section is me taking a swipe at the pan-Indian buffet of
		holy certitudes that compete like bad biryani stalls—each insisting its
		spices are divine while giving you the same dysentery. I grew up in a
		Bengali household where gods were mostly decorative, something my mother
		kept around like cheap, fragile clay toys that could scold me for not studying
		enough. But even then, I noticed the pattern: every community loudly
		insisting that their god’s beard lice were medicinal and the other god’s
		lice were toxic. As a child who barely understood why my own genitalia
		seemed smaller than my classmates’, divine rivalries felt like the grown-up
		version of the same insecurity—holy pissing contests, literally.
	</p>
	<p>
		The “Christian south” line is not theology; it’s my memory of traveling to South India and realizing that holiness everywhere is the same fragrance layered
		over decay—like Dettol poured into an overflowing drain. You can hear
		the accents change, but the logic stays the same: blame god for everything,
		weaponize god for everything, sterilize your hatreds with a hymn. That trip to South India was the only trip with my family that I can hold on to. 
	</p>
	<p>
		When I say “you want to cry but you well up with pee,” it’s because most of
		my emotional breakthroughs until age twelve happened standing in front of
		a urinal too tall for me, trying not to get splashback on my shoes. The
		world around me was violent, unequal, caste-coded, god-soaked, and I was
		this puny boy wondering if something was biologically wrong with me. That
		same anxiety now returns when I watch political mobs use holiness as a
		pretext to target others. Hatred becomes a hobby, a pilgrimage, a
		deodorized duty.
	</p>
	<p>
		There’s a disturbing line, “holy is why you live,”
		because I’ve known people whose entire purpose—people I’ve loved—became
		finding someone weaker to shame or someone different to despise. That’s
		the tragedy: the rotten holiness that promises transcendence but delivers
		only inherited cruelty. And I wrote it with the same grief I had when I
		watched my classmates giggle at the kid whose lunch smelled too humble or
		whose shirt was always half-untucked. Shame is contagious here; holiness
		is just the handrail we all grab so we don’t slip.
	</p>
</Tbx>

<Tbx>
	<p>
		This stanza came from a lifetime of watching India move with all the speed
		and grace of a snail suffering from chronic gastric distress. I spent my
		childhood waiting—waiting for teachers, for buses, for rickshaws, for
		government offices to open after chai, for people to stop lecturing and
		start doing anything remotely useful. I think I realized by age nine that
		this nation is built on delay, the way a termite mound is built from spit
		and soil. We are a gastropod civilization: soft interior, thick shell,
		endless slime trails of bureaucracy.
	</p>
	<p>
		“Holy hollow shell” isn't just mockery; it’s autobiographical longing. I
		grew up in a home where books were stacked high but the world outside was
		an empty shell pretending to contain meaning—Durga Puja lights trying to make up for
		gaping potholes, political processions covering policy decay, the smell of
		ghee masking the stench of nearby drains. As a kid, when I’d sit on the
		terrace with my myopic eyes squinting at the city’s chaotic horizon, I
		already knew something was hollow here. Something astonishingly fundamental—introspective honesty.
	</p>
	<p>
		The smoke-through-ass line is pure George Carlin filtered through
		North-Calcutta adolescent cynicism. Carlin had rage; I had sewer water
		seeping into my shoes during monsoon. Together they create this strange
		hybrid humor that tries to survive the absurdity of a country that
		choreographs incompetence and calls it culture. I don’t hate India—I hate
		that India refuses to evolve while claiming to have evolved since the
		Vedas. You can’t have both, but India demands both, the way a child demands
		love for a drawing that’s mostly scribbles.
	</p>
</Tbx>

	<p>
		This whole long section is me finally letting go of the polite Bengali
		habit of tiptoeing around power structures—because power here is always
		disguised with some deity’s costume jewelry. The childish “bee, J, pee”
		joke is precisely the point: this massive propaganda behemoth is reduced
		to a pun you’d hear on a school staircase. It’s the same instinct I had
		when I learned to draw hairy triangles and dangling circles on textbook
		margins—strip the thing of its authority to see it naked.
	</p>
	<p>
		When I say “Vishnu’s avatar fish market,” I’m remembering the Calcutta
		fish markets I grew up passing—hulking men arguing about hilsa prices,
		women negotiating with the sharpness of a blade, the smell of river mud
		mingling with ambition. Indian politics is exactly that, except it has
		the gall to pretend it’s descended from the Upanishads. It’s the same
		fraudulent dignity as a man adjusting his sacred thread while scratching
		his armpit.
	</p>
	<p>
		The stanzas about hospitals, schools, food, starvation—that comes from a
		very personal ache. I spent my childhood in a city where getting sick was
		a gamble, where education was a bureaucratic punishment, where every
		aspiration required fighting both poverty and superstition. I’ve watched
		people I loved die not because of karma or destiny but because the system
		could not be bothered. And now the same system sells us god-approved
		bullet points as policy.
	</p>
	<p>
		“Promise postponed” is something I felt in my bones even before adulthood.
		Every teacher promised enlightenment next class, every politician promised
		a better city next term, every god promised justice next life. My life has
		been a queue of delayed deliveries—Amazon Prime, but for disappointment.
	</p>
	<p>
		And the “son-in-law” line—believe me, I wrote that with the memory of
		watching uncles, godmen, businessmen, netas, journalists all exchanging
		wink-wink alliances at weddings, funerals, pujas. India is run like a
		giant extended family where you’re the malnourished kid sent to fetch
		sugar while the adults drink themselves into hypocrisy. Nepotism here may be a minister's affection; but it’s corruption for the millions who don’t even know they’re
		being fed to their “god” in installments as "prasad". 
	</p>

	<p>
		I lose it here—All the
		lingering politeness is gone, the years of academic restraint, the American-style
		critical thinking I tried to graft onto my Bengali bones—everything gave
		way to a kind of obscene honesty that felt truer than any editorial
		column I ever read. India is full of loud, self-important pests—anchors,
		dynasts, godwomen, gurus, corporate astrologers—people who speak with the
		certainty of prophets and the intellect of cockroaches. I grew up watching
		them and thinking the problem was me. Turns out the problem was them.
	</p>
	<p>
		“This is why the government of the fucks” is deeply personal. As a
		melanin-rich, myopic, short Bengali boy who thought books would save him,
		I didn’t realize my entire education system was being hollowed out by
		people who couldn’t differentiate between mythology and history, who
		thought Darwin was optional and astronomy was identical to astrology.
		That’s the betrayal: not that politicians lie but that they sabotage the
		very tools—the schools, the libraries, the science labs—that could have
		saved someone like me growing up in this country. 
	</p>
	<p>
		The hope is dipped-in-acid. I want to believe
		in hope; I really do. I believed in it when I left India for the US, when
		I returned, when I tried to build something new, when I wrote this blog.
		But hope here is weaponized, sold like expired biscuits at a kirana store,
		promoted as a national sedative to keep the masses docile. The cruelest
		truth is that each party offers a different flavor of the same sewage.
	</p>
	<p>
		The sexual metaphors here—turd, fart, diarrhea—they are childish on the
		surface but philosophically appropriate. Every society has a sewage system;
		ours just speaks the loudest and thinks it’s poetry. My tenderness lies in
		the fact that I know most people don’t choose the sewage—they’re born into
		it, marinated in it, told it’s holy water. I’ve been that person. I still
		am, sometimes.
	</p>
	<p>
		So the final promise—“we’ll really shit on you”—is the only honest line
		Indian politics has ever offered. Beneath the profanity is a boy who grew
		up trying to escape his shame through knowledge, who then discovered the
		knowledge was itself drowning in a swamp of superstition and jingoistic
		fantasy. This blog, this doggerel, this feral commentary—they’re my way of
		bailing water with a broken cup, knowing full well the boat is sinking but
		also knowing I can at least describe the drowning clearly.
	</p>
