---
title : "My father and books" 

date : June 11 2008
---

An open mind is a precondition to an open heart. I don't quite remember where I read that quote, but I almost agree with it. I have seen people brag about their own generosity only to come out as not so compassionate. This is not something new. I have seen charity not begin at home—and most often, not begin at all. I saw that in my maternal relatives and around me, growing up in the 70s. I saw people choke their kindness based on caste or frivolous anecdotal hearsay.

I guess my father was an exception, at least when it came to his family. He always put his family first, before himself. My father was a constant in the variable fortunes of his familial poverty, and although we never were in abject poverty, he made us rehearsed and ready. When my grandfather migrated from Rangoon, Burma, he lost his job, and my father—then eleven—had to step up to the role of breadwinner for a family of two brothers (excluding him), four sisters, and his parents. Later, that rehearsal he perfected in his early teens, for keeping his family fed, became his—and consequently, and unfortunately, our—life, in spite of his income. We lived as if we didn't have enough of whatever was the matter in question, from rice to beans. Every material possession or amenity was ornamental and came at a price—usually paid by my mother's vocal insistence and retaliations. The undemocratic and economically impressionistic drama was intense, and for the fear of it, I withdrew from wants to the extent that I really don't have any material wants anymore—or if I can get it, I fear it, without really knowing why. I don't go out of the way to repress my wants, but owing to the sheer lack of attachment to possessions, I have an odd respect, love, and hate for things, all at the same time.

In my childhood, my only escape from the internal guilt of wanting even the most basic—the uncouth outcome of repressed wants—was my imagination. I didn't have many toys. I knew even if they weren't always expensive, they symbolized wealth and would bring undue stress into the household. Books were the one luxury that had a neutral status symbol, and it was an extension of my father's bibliophilic curiosity. I never saw him read a book except excerpts from the *Gita* as part of his obsessive morning rituals. My mother first taught me how to read before I was admitted to school, and I was soon reading every comic, magazine, and book that I could lay my eyes on. Not only did I like reading books—I almost lived in them, to the extent that a large amount of what I knew about the world was through the colored lenses of the authors, and mostly fiction. Fiction was stranger and closer to me than reality because it came in print. Reality was a grown-up world of contradiction—far too fearsome and loathsome for me.

I grew a fascination for dictionaries, which equipped me to read what I wanted to read. I still stayed limited to what was around me, and despised classics for the very snobby company they kept. In a strict sense, it was the love of books that was the only shared interest between me and my father. My fondest memories of him are those at the book fairs in Calcutta, where he would spend beyond his means. And although he was himself robbed of the pleasure of reading, he vicariously enjoyed my immersion in that world. He would laboriously count all the pages of a new book to make sure they were there, and make book jackets from old newspapers. After I had roughed them up, the books would get clinical attention from him, and he would diligently repair them. His bookbinding—like his other repairs—wasn't entirely functional, but I liked the attention. It was almost as if he was paying some attention to me.
